Notes on LLM-judge bias: what survives a paired test

Feb 8, 2026 · 11 min read

Over the back half of 2025 I ran a sustained comparison of three frontier judge models (GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 4.6, and Gemini 2.5 Pro) against a panel of three human raters, on a held-out set of 2,400 single-turn QA pairs from four domains. This isn't a paper; it's a long working note. Numbers are approximate and the dataset is going up on GitHub when I'm done arguing with a reviewer.

The setup

  • Each QA pair was scored by all three judges and by three humans, blind to each other.
  • Scoring rubric was a 5-point scale with anchored descriptions, plus a "would you actually trust this answer" yes/no.
  • Position randomized when the judge saw paired answers (the most common eval mode in industry).
  • 2,400 pairs sounds like a lot but is barely enough for the cross-cuts I wanted to do; the public release will be 12k.

Three biases survived statistical scrutiny

1. Length bias is real and large

All three judges preferred longer answers, controlling for content quality. The effect was strongest in Gemini, weakest in Claude, but never small. A 30%-longer answer with identical factual content was ~14 points more likely to win a paired comparison. Humans showed a smaller version of this (~5 points), but the human effect mostly disappeared when raters were told to "weight content over presentation."

2. Position bias is real and asymmetric

When the same answer was presented as A or B, judges varied their score. GPT-4o slightly favored position A (4 points); Gemini and Claude slightly favored position B. Always randomize, and report results averaged across orderings.

3. Self-preference is real but smaller than people claim

The much-cited "judges prefer their own outputs" effect was present but modest in this dataset: roughly 6–8 points across all three judges. Notably, GPT-4o did not show the strongest self-preference; Gemini did. The takeaway isn't "never use a judge from the same family as the generator," but "always disclose it and quantify it."

Three biases didn't survive

  • Sycophancy on rationale. Asking the judge to explain before scoring (vs. score first) didn't change scores meaningfully in this dataset, contradicting some earlier work. Possibly an artifact of newer models being better calibrated.
  • Refusal bias. Judges did not systematically punish answers that politely declined out-of-scope questions, once we controlled for length. This was the most useful finding for the day job.
  • "First number wins." The hypothesis that judges anchor on the first numerical claim in an answer didn't hold under paired analysis.

The headline finding I keep coming back to

Inter-judge agreement (across the three frontier models) was higher than inter-human agreement on the same task. That sounds great until you realize what it means: the judges agree with each other more than humans do, because they share systematic biases that humans don't share. High inter-rater agreement isn't a quality signal when the raters were trained on overlapping corpora.

For now: use multiple judges from different families, randomize position, control for length, and don't trust a metric where the judge can't show its work.
